## What is a Caesar Cipher?

The Caesar cipher is one of the simplest and most widely known encryption techniques. It's a type of **substitution cipher** where each letter in the plaintext is replaced by a letter some fixed number of positions down the alphabet.

For example, with a shift of 3:
- A becomes D
- B becomes E
- C becomes F
- ... and so on

## History

The Caesar cipher is named after Julius Caesar, who apparently used it to communicate with his generals. According to Suetonius, Caesar used a shift of 3, but the general technique works with any shift value.

## How It Works

The encryption process is straightforward:

1. Choose a shift value (also called the key), typically between 1 and 25
2. For each letter in your message:
   - Find its position in the alphabet (A=0, B=1, ... Z=25)
   - Add the shift value
   - Wrap around if necessary (after Z comes A)
   - Replace with the letter at the new position

### Example

Let's encrypt "HELLO" with a shift of 3:

- H (7) → K (10)
- E (4) → H (7)
- L (11) → O (14)
- L (11) → O (14)
- O (14) → R (17)

Result: **KHOOR**

## Decryption

To decrypt a Caesar cipher, simply reverse the process:
- Use the negative of the shift value (or shift in the opposite direction)
- For a shift of 3, decrypt by shifting -3 (or +23)

## Breaking the Caesar Cipher

The Caesar cipher is extremely easy to break because:

1. **Limited keyspace**: Only 25 possible shifts (26 if you include 0, which doesn't change anything)
2. **Brute force**: You can try all 25 shifts in seconds
3. **Frequency analysis**: Letter frequencies remain recognizable

### Brute Force Attack

Since there are only 25 possible shifts, you can simply try them all:

```
Ciphertext: KHOOR
Shift 1: JGNNQ
Shift 2: IFMMP
Shift 3: HELLO ← Found it!
...
```

When you see recognizable English words, you've found the right shift!

## Variations

- **ROT13**: A special case with a shift of 13. Since the alphabet has 26 letters, applying ROT13 twice returns the original text
- **Negative shifts**: Shifting left instead of right
- **Number shifts**: Some versions include numbers in the alphabet

## Weaknesses

The Caesar cipher should **never be used for real security** because:
- Only 25 possible keys (trivial to brute force)
- Vulnerable to frequency analysis
- Pattern preservation (double letters stay double)
- No key distribution needed (also a weakness!)
